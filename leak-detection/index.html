Leak detection is the work of locating a hidden water loss before anything is opened up, using sound, heat, moisture and pressure so that the repair happens in one place instead of across a whole room or floor. Homes here are often 50 to 90 years old, so original pipe tends to be what runs behind walls and under floors.

High water bill? Run the meter test before paying for anything

The Village meters your water, so a leak shows up as a number that keeps climbing. A bill that creeps upward for months, or jumps in one cycle, is the usual first clue. You can confirm it yourself.

Twenty minutes at the meter

  • 1Stop all use. Close every tap, make sure no one flushes, and pause the washer, dishwasher, ice maker, humidifier and any irrigation.
  • 2Read the meter. Find it, usually where the water line enters the house. Many have a small dial or triangle that spins with any flow. Write down the numbers.
  • 3Wait and recheck. After 15 to 30 minutes, read it again. Movement means water is going somewhere.
  • 4Close the house main valve. If the meter stops, the leak is inside the house. If it keeps moving, the leak is on the pipe between the meter and that valve.
  • 5Rule out the toilets. Drop a little food coloring into each tank. If color reaches the bowl without flushing, a flapper is leaking.

Slab leak signs: what the floor and the house tell you

A slab leak is a pipe leaking under or inside a concrete floor. A house on a slab, a basement floor or an addition poured on a slab can all show the same signs.

A warm spot underfoot

A patch of floor that feels warm points to a hot water line leaking beneath it.

Running water, nothing on

A faint rush or hiss at night with every tap off is worth taking seriously.

A meter that keeps turning

Movement with the house shut down is the strongest clue, and the meter test above confirms it.

Hot water that arrives late

A hot line losing water into the ground can slow the supply and keep the heater working harder.

Damp flooring or lifted edges

Wet carpet, cracked tile or a swollen baseboard suggests water from below.

Hidden leaks in walls and ceilings: where the water travels before it shows

The stain on a ceiling is rarely directly below the leak. Water runs along a joist, a pipe or the back of the drywall before it finds a place to drip, so a kitchen ceiling can be wet from a bathroom a few feet away. Our plumbers trace the path backward instead of cutting at the stain.

We check likely sources first: a shower pan or tub overflow, supply connections under a fixture, a drain trap, and a vent pipe. A moisture meter maps damp areas without damage, and a thermal camera shows cool or warm patches that follow a pipe run.

When the source sits behind finished drywall, a small inspection opening is sometimes the only way to confirm it. We keep it small and the patching is straightforward. Painting and finishing belong to another trade.

Different leaks call for different tools, and most searches use more than one. Here is what each does and does not tell us.

Outside and underground: leaks between the street and the house

A leak in the buried line shows itself differently. Look for a soggy or sunken patch of lawn, a strip of grass greener than the rest, a damp spot by the foundation, or water at the surface when it has not rained. Pressure that drops through the whole house is another hint.

Utilities do not mark private lines, so we find the path ourselves before listening along it. Once located, repair is a separate step, covered under water line repair.

The water heater: the leak that looks like something else

A puddle near the tank is not always the tank. A dripping drain valve, a loose supply or hot outlet fitting, a T&P valve discharging, or condensation on a cold pipe can all pool beneath it. We check these first before calling the tank itself finished.

A tank that has rusted through is a different story, and replacement is the honest answer. See water heater repair for the checks we run on those.

Where finding the leak stops and opening up starts

Detection narrows the search, often to a small area, but it never guarantees an exact spot. Sometimes a small hole in drywall, tile or a slab is the only way to confirm. We keep access holes small. Tile, concrete and paint finishing is its own trade.
