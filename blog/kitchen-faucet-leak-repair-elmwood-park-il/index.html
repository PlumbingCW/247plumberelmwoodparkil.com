Kitchen Faucet Leaking? Find the Source Before You Buy Parts

Where the water appears tells you which part has failed. Dry the faucet, watch it, and the leak usually points to its own cause.

By the Elmwood Park 24/7 Plumbing teamPublished October 29, 2026Updated October 29, 2026

Dry the faucet with a towel. Then watch it for a few minutes. Where the first bead of water appears is the whole diagnosis, and it saves you from replacing parts that were never broken.

A kitchen faucet leaks in four places: under the sink, around the base, at the handle and at the spout. Each one has its own cause and its own fix. This guide takes them in order, then helps you decide whether the faucet deserves repair at all in your Elmwood Park kitchen.

Four leak locations and their usual causes

Match the drip to the part
Where it leaksLikely culpritTypical fix
Under the sinkLoose supply line nut, worn washer or a split hoseTighten or replace supply lines and fittings
Around the base of the faucetWorn O-rings or a loose mounting nutReplace O-rings, snug the mount from below
At the handleWorn cartridge, stem seal or loose handleReplace the cartridge or seals
From the spout tipWorn cartridge, valve seat or ball assemblyRebuild or replace the internal valve
From a pull-out hoseCracked hose or loose connectorReplace the hose or connector

Notice that two of these are cheap, quick jobs and two require opening the faucet body. That is why finding the source first matters.

Start under the cabinet, where most leaks hide

Before you take the faucet apart, rule out the plumbing beneath it. A faucet that seems to leak from above sometimes has water running down from a supply connection and dripping off the base.

Under-sink inspection

  • 1Dry every fitting. Wipe the shutoff valves, supply lines and connections with a paper towel.
  • 2Run the water and feel along the lines. A damp towel or fingertip finds a drip faster than your eyes.
  • 3Check the shutoff valves. Older valves can weep at the stem or the nut. Tighten gently, a quarter turn at a time.
  • 4Inspect the supply lines. Braided lines can rust at the ends or kink. Replace any that look worn.
  • 5Look at the cabinet floor. Staining, swelling or a musty smell means the leak has been going on for a while.

Leaking at the base or handle: what is inside

Most modern faucets keep their working parts in a single cartridge or valve. A leak at the handle or base means something in that stack has worn, a seal, an O-ring, or the cartridge itself. Shutting the water off at the stops below the sink before you open anything is non-negotiable.

The usual routine is to remove the handle cap, loosen the set screw, lift the handle, and pull out the cartridge or retaining nut. Photograph each layer so reassembly goes in the right order. Some brands use a cartridge puller, a small specialty tool, to extract a stuck cartridge without damaging the body.

Do not force it. A faucet body that cracks because someone prised too hard has become a replacement.

Cartridge, ball and ceramic disc valves in plain terms

Faucets control water with one of a few internal designs. You do not need to memorize them, but knowing which you have helps you buy the right part.

Cartridge or ceramic disc

  • Single lever or two handles, smooth action
  • Usually a sealed insert that replaces as a unit
  • Ceramic discs resist wear but dislike grit
  • Often the simplest repair if parts are available

Ball or older compression

  • Ball designs use a rotating ball with springs and seats
  • Compression stems squeeze a rubber washer to stop flow
  • More small parts, so repair kits matter
  • Older faucets may be hard to find parts for

Bring the old part to the supplier, or note the brand and model stamped on the faucet. Matching parts is half the battle.

Spout drips and low pressure from one common cause

A spout that keeps dripping after the handle is off is usually a worn cartridge or valve seat. A spout that sprays weakly or sideways is often a clogged aerator, the small screen on the tip. Unscrew it, rinse out the debris and soak it in vinegar if mineral crust has formed.

If cleaning the aerator does not fix the flow, and a new cartridge does not stop the drip, the faucet body itself may be pitted or worn. At that point, replacing the faucet beats repeated repairs.

Hidden leaks that look like a faucet problem

A cabinet that stays damp after you fixed the faucet may have another source. Supply stops, a dishwasher connection, the drain trap and even condensation on cold pipes can be responsible. If you cannot find the drip or the damage keeps returning, a professional leak detection visit uses moisture meters and listening tools to trace water behind walls and under floors.

Never ignore soft cabinet bottoms or mold smells. Constant dampness rots wood and invites pests, so a small leak left for weeks costs more than the faucet.

Faucet leaks and your water in Elmwood Park

Tap water here is Chicago's Lake Michigan water, bought by the Village, chlorinated and pumped to homes. Crust building up around an aerator or faucet base points to minerals, but we cannot cite a hardness figure, so test your tap or read the Village's published water report.

The pipe feeding your kitchen also matters. The Village has been mapping what its service lines are made of for state lead-line rules, and it wants owners and tenants to fill in its material inventory form. If a contractor ever proposes swapping only a piece of a service line, ask questions, since state law generally bars partial lead line replacement.
