Water-Powered Backup Sump Pumps for Elmwood Park, IL Basements

In July 2011, rain of 2 to 4 inches an hour left several feet of water on Oak Leaf Avenue. A backup pump is meant for nights like that, and there is more than one kind.

By the Elmwood Park 24/7 Plumbing teamPublished October 29, 2026Updated October 29, 2026

Storms that knock out power are the same storms that fill sump pits fastest, which is why a primary pump alone leaves a gap. A backup that does not depend on your electric service closes that gap. One option does it with nothing more than the water pressure already in your pipes.

That is the idea behind a water-powered backup sump pump. This guide explains how one works, where it shines, where it falls short, how it compares with a battery backup, and what to check with the Village before anything is connected to your plumbing in Elmwood Park.

How a water-powered backup actually works

A water-powered unit hangs in the sump pit above the water line, beside the main pump. It connects to your home's cold water supply. When the pit rises past the backup's float, a valve opens and municipal water is forced through a narrow nozzle and a venturi, a tapered passage that speeds the flow and creates suction.

That suction pulls pit water up and out through the same outlet pipe, mixing it with the supply water as it goes. The more supply water you push through, the more pit water you move. Nothing in it needs electricity, a charged battery or a motor, and that is the whole appeal.

The case for it, and the case against

What it does well

  • Works through power outages with no battery to charge or replace.
  • Few moving parts, so there is not much to wear out.
  • Can run for as long as water pressure holds, with no battery limit.
  • Sits quietly in the pit until it is needed.

What to weigh carefully

  • Uses a lot of supply water while it runs, and you pay for what the meter records.
  • Needs adequate water pressure, so low pressure means poor performance.
  • Moves less pit water than a good primary pump or a strong battery pump.
  • Needs a proper connection to the supply, with backflow protection, where local rules require it.

The supply water and the pit water leave together through the pit's outlet. Pressure from a private well is a poor match, while a municipal supply is the usual setting, though local rules still come first.

Check connection and discharge rules before you buy

A water-powered backup ties your drinking water line to a pit that holds groundwater, so backflow protection is not optional. Plumbing codes generally address this, but rules about connections and where the discharge may go vary, and we cannot assume anything is allowed for your address.

Before installing one, ask the Village whether the connection, the backflow device and the discharge route are acceptable. Ask rather than assume. Our sump pump repair and installation team can install the plumbing once you know the answer.

Also confirm where the outlet leads. Discharging to a lawn, a sanitary line or a storm line each carries its own rules, and sending clear water into the wrong line can create problems for you or neighbors.

Testing any backup, so it works when you need it

1

Test the primary pump first

Pour a few buckets of water into the pit and watch the pump start, run and shut off cleanly. Listen for grinding or a motor that hums without moving water, and check that the float swings freely instead of catching on the pit wall or the cord.

2

Test the backup on its own

For a water-powered unit, lift the float or let the pit fill until it starts. Watch it pull water down.

3

Check the outlet

Make sure the discharge pipe is clear, the check valve is working and water leaves the house without returning.

4

Set a reminder

Test every few months and before storm season. A backup you have never tested is a hope, not a plan. Write the date on the pit lid or in your phone so the next test does not slip.

Signs your basement protection is not enough

A pump can look fine and still be undersized or worn out, and a pump that has run for many years is worth having a plumber inspect before the wet season.

Pump runs constantly in wet weather

It may be undersized or the check valve may be failing.

Pit water level rises despite the pump running

Flow in may exceed flow out.

Rust, odd noises or a burning smell

Replace the motor before it quits mid-storm.

Water at the floor drain, not the pit

That may be sewer backup, which a sump pump does not stop.

Elmwood Park flooding, combined sewers and your pit

Sewers here are combined, meaning rain runoff and household waste travel in one pipe, and a heavy storm can push water back up into basements. A sump pump handles groundwater around your foundation, but it does not stop sewer backup, which is a different problem needing a backwater valve or overhead sewer.

The area has a flood history. FEMA flooding declarations came in 2007, 2008 and 2011. In July 2011, several feet of water stood on Oak Leaf Avenue and cars stalled on 80th Avenue. A flood mitigation project to reduce sewer backups was completed in 2015, but heavy storms can still overwhelm local systems.

The Village has run a flood-proofing assistance program that shares costs on protective upgrades, and its form says inspectors will review an existing system. Ask the building department whether the program is open now and what it covers today. Also ask your insurer about sewer backup coverage.
