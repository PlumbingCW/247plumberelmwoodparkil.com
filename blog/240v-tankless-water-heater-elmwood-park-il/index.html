220 Volt Tankless Water Heater: What 240V Electric Needs in Elmwood Park, IL

A 220 volt tankless unit promises endless hot water, but it asks a lot of your home's electrical system. Here is what to know before you buy one.

By the Elmwood Park 24/7 Plumbing teamPublished October 29, 2026Updated October 29, 2026

Search for a 220 volt tankless water heater and you will find compact wall units with big claims. Strip away the marketing and the question comes down to something simple: does your house have the electrical capacity to run one, and will it deliver enough hot water at the temperature you expect?

In everyday talk, 220 and 240 volt mean the same supply, the one larger appliances use. This guide, written for Elmwood Park homeowners, explains what an electric tankless heater needs, how flow and cold winter water affect it, and when gas tankless or a tank heater is the smarter choice.

What 220 and 240 volts mean for a water heater

Most outlets in your house deliver 120 volts. Large electric appliances such as dryers, ovens and some water heaters run on a 240 volt supply, which many people casually call 220. It is the same service with a different label, and it needs its own wiring from the panel.

An electric tankless heater heats water as it flows through, using powerful elements instead of storing it in a tank. That is why it needs so much electrical capacity at once. The electrical side of the job belongs to a licensed electrician, not to a plumber and certainly not to a weekend project.

What an electric tankless install asks of your home

Before anyone talks about a model, these three things decide whether electric tankless is realistic for your house.

1

A dedicated circuit

The unit generally needs its own circuit from the panel and is not shared with other loads. A licensed electrician confirms what your unit requires, and the electrician does the wiring.

2

Spare capacity in the electrical service

Older homes can have a main panel that is already close to its limit. An electrician can tell you whether the service can carry another large load, or whether an upgrade is part of the project.

3

Water piping and clearances

Our plumbers handle the water connections, shutoff valves, isolation valves for flushing, and a safe path for the pressure relief discharge. The wall needs room for the unit and for access to service it.

Flow rate versus temperature rise, in plain words

A tankless heater is not rated by gallons stored. It is judged by how much water it can heat per minute and by how many degrees it can raise the temperature. These two numbers pull against each other.

Winter water makes the biggest difference

Water coming into a Chicago-area home in January is much colder than in July. Because a tankless heater has to raise the temperature of whatever arrives, winter is when it works hardest. A unit that handles one shower in summer may barely keep up with two showers in a cold snap.

That is why sizing should be based on your coldest water and your busiest hour, not on average days. Households with several bathrooms, teens, or heavy laundry habits should ask for a sizing discussion before choosing a unit. A good plumber will ask how many fixtures typically run together.

When electric tankless fits, and when something else is smarter

Electric tankless tends to suit

  • A small household with limited simultaneous hot water use.
  • A home with an electrical panel that has room and a licensed electrician on board.
  • Wall space where a gas line and venting would be hard to run.
  • A secondary bathroom or an addition far from the main heater.

Gas tankless or a tank heater tends to suit

  • A larger household where several fixtures often run together.
  • A home where the electrical service is already near its limit.
  • A basement with an existing gas line and suitable venting, often the easier swap in older homes.
  • Anyone who wants the simplest like-for-like replacement of an old tank.

None of these is universally better. The right choice depends on your panel, your gas supply, your habits and your budget. Our water heater replacement team can compare tank and tankless options with you.

Permits, inspections and who does what

Plumbing and electrical work are treated separately by the Village, so a tankless installation can involve two permits and two inspections, one for each trade. Requirements differ from one job to the next, so check with the Village building department about your specific project before work starts.

The electrician has separate licensing from the plumber. Ask both trades who pulls which permit and how inspections get scheduled, since inspections need advance notice.

Maintenance signs an electric tankless needs attention

Electric units have no burner, but they still need care over time. Watch for these.

Lukewarm water that used to run hot

Scale on the heating elements or filters can cut performance.

Error codes or a unit that shuts off

Treat codes as information, and check the manual before assuming a failure.

Water temperature swings

A sign that flow, scale or a sensor may be off.

Leaks at the connections

Shut the water off to the unit and call a plumber.
