How to Remove Push-to-Connect Plumbing Fittings Safely

A push-to-connect fitting looks like it should come off with a hard pull. It will not, and forcing it is how a small job becomes a wet floor.

By the Elmwood Park 24/7 Plumbing teamPublished October 29, 2026Updated October 29, 2026

Push-to-connect fittings, often called by the SharkBite brand name, grip a pipe with a stainless steel ring of teeth and seal it with a rubber O-ring. You push the pipe in and it holds. Taking one off is a different matter, because the same grip that makes it fast to install makes it stubborn to remove without the right release.

This guide walks through the release in a way a careful homeowner in Elmwood Park, IL can follow, then covers pipe prep, reuse, where these fittings fail and where they do not belong. Where a repair needs a licensed plumber, we say so plainly.

Before you touch the fitting: what to confirm

Identify what the fitting is joining. Push-fit connectors are made for copper, CPVC and PEX, and some for polyethylene. If the pipe is corroded galvanized steel, a gas line or an unknown material, stop here. These fittings are not a repair for those, and a plumber should look at it.

Next, find out how the line is fed. A fitting on a branch off the water heater is a very different job from one on the line that feeds the whole house. Know where your main shutoff is before you start, and test it, because an old valve that will not close is the most common surprise.

  • Turn off power or gas to the water heater before working near it, since draining a line can run the tank dry.
  • Lay towels and a bucket under the fitting and keep a flashlight handy.
  • Check whether the pipe is hot. Hot water lines can scald for a while after the heater shuts off.

Releasing the fitting in five steps

1

Depressurize and drain

Close the shutoff that feeds the line. Open the lowest faucet to drain the pipe and a high one to let air in, then wait until the flow stops. A fitting pulled under pressure can spray or blow the pipe out.

2

Position the release

Slide a disconnect tool or the plastic disconnect clip over the pipe and against the collet, the colored ring on the face of the fitting. The release must sit square, not at an angle.

3

Press the collet in evenly

Push the tool toward the fitting body. This presses the collet in and lifts the grip teeth off the pipe. Do not pry or twist with pliers, which bends the collet and often damages the pipe.

4

Pull the pipe straight out

While holding the collet in, pull the pipe straight back. Expect a little water from the line and the fitting. If it fights you, the pipe may be deformed or corroded, so stop and check before forcing.

5

Inspect both ends

Look at the pipe end for deep scratches, flats, rust or green corrosion and at the fitting for cracks or a pinched O-ring. Anything damaged means a new fitting and a trimmed pipe at the least.

Pipe prep: cut, deburr and mark the depth

The grip teeth score the pipe, so the last inch or so of a removed pipe is usually scarred. Cut it back past the scratches with a tubing cutter, which leaves a clean square end. A hacksaw leaves a ragged cut that can slice the new O-ring during insertion.

Deburr the inside and outside edge with a deburring tool or fine file. Burrs are small but they are the most common reason a push-fit joint weeps. Wipe the end clean and check that it is round and smooth.

Then mark the insertion depth. Most fittings include a depth gauge, or you can use the depth chart for the fitting size. Pencil a line on the pipe, push the pipe in until the line meets the collet, and give it a gentle tug to confirm it grabbed. A joint that stops short of the mark has not seated.

PEX needs a stiffener

A stainless insert goes inside a PEX tube end so the O-ring has something firm to seal against. Skipping it is a classic cause of slow leaks.

How a push-fit joint tells you it is failing

Failures are rarely dramatic. They start small, and a quick look can catch them.

A bead of water at the collet

Moisture that returns after you wipe it dry means the O-ring is not sealing.

Green or white crust

Mineral crust or green corrosion on a copper pipe near the fitting shows a slow leak.

A fitting that moves

If the pipe can be wiggled or pulled with light pressure, it never seated or the grip is worn.

Dampness on nearby framing

Stains below the fitting often show up before drips are visible. A leak detection visit can trace where the water starts.

A faint hiss under pressure

A fine spray or hiss means the seal is breached. Close the shutoff and call.

Can you reuse a removed fitting?

Some manufacturers state that their fittings can be released and reused if you use the correct disconnect tool and the O-ring and collet are undamaged. Others advise replacing them. The instructions printed for your specific model are the authority, so read them.

A reasonable rule of thumb is to reuse a fitting only if it came off cleanly on a pipe that is still sound, and replace it any time the pipe was scarred, the O-ring looks flattened or the joint has been wet.
