Whole House Tankless Water Heaters in Elmwood Park: What to Check Before You Buy

A tankless unit heats water as you use it, so the size of the house matters less than how many things run at once and how cold the incoming water is.

By the Elmwood Park 24/7 Plumbing teamPublished October 29, 2026Updated October 29, 2026

Picture a Saturday morning in a two-bath house. One person is showering, the dishwasher is running and someone starts the washing machine. A tank heater handles that by pulling from a stored reserve. A tankless heater has no reserve, so it has to heat everything on the spot.

That single difference drives every decision in this guide, from sizing to fuel choice to what an older Elmwood Park house needs behind the wall. Read it as a buyer's checklist, and keep a plumber in the loop before you commit to a model.

Tank or tankless: what actually changes in the house

Neither type is better in every home. They solve the same problem with different trade-offs.

Traditional tank

  • Keeps a reserve of heated water ready
  • Simpler installation, usually fewer upgrades
  • Standby heat loss while the tank sits full
  • Runs out when demand outlasts the reserve
  • Takes up more floor space

Tankless

  • Heats water only when a tap opens
  • Wall-mounted and compact
  • Water supply never runs out of stored volume
  • Heavy demands on gas supply, venting or electric service
  • Needs periodic descaling to keep its flow

For a household that wants endless hot showers and has the utilities to support it, tankless can make sense. For a house where the gas line, vent path or electrical panel would need major work, a high-quality tank may serve better. Our tankless water heater team can look at both options on site.

Sizing begins with what runs at the same time

Forget square footage. Tankless sizing comes down to two questions. How many hot water fixtures could be running together on your busiest morning, and how much does the water need to be warmed on its way through?

The second question is called temperature rise, the gap between incoming cold water and the temperature you want at the tap. A bigger gap means the heater has to work harder, which lowers the flow it can deliver. Manufacturers publish flow ratings at different rises, and a plumber reads those charts against your real fixtures rather than trusting a headline number.

Write down your simultaneous uses before you shop. A shower, a kitchen faucet and a washing machine tend to be the combination that stresses a unit. Low-flow showerheads reduce the load. Rain showers and body sprays raise it sharply.

Gas line capacity and venting in a house built decades ago

A tankless gas heater can demand far more gas at peak than the tank it replaces. An older house may have a modest gas pipe feeding the furnace, range and dryer, with little to spare. The plumber measures the run from the meter, counts every appliance and decides whether the existing pipe can carry the added load or needs upsizing.

Venting is the second hurdle. High-efficiency units use plastic vent pipe that runs through an exterior wall or the roof, and the termination point must stay clear of windows, vents and walkways. Older flues made for standard tanks are usually not suitable. Combustion air matters too, because a heater starved of air burns poorly and can produce carbon monoxide.

Install a carbon monoxide alarm near any gas appliance and test it regularly. Never block a vent or seal a utility room tight around a burner.

Gas smell? Do not stay and investigate

If you smell gas at any point, leave the building at once. Do not touch switches or use your phone inside. Call the gas utility and 911 from outside.

Winter water is the number that surprises people

In the Chicago area, incoming cold water gets noticeably colder in winter. A tankless unit that kept up fine in October may deliver less flow in January because it has to lift the water through a larger temperature rise. Pick a unit sized for the coldest months rather than the mild ones.

If a manufacturer's chart shows flow dropping at higher temperature rises, take that seriously. Sizing for the worst day is cheaper than apologizing to your family every winter.

Questions to settle before ordering a unit

Pre-purchase checklist

  • 1Your peak demand. List every hot water fixture likely to run at once and match it to the unit's flow rating at winter temperature rise.
  • 2Fuel and supply. Confirm gas pipe size and meter capacity, or panel space for electric. Ask for it in writing from the installer.
  • 3Vent route. Walk the path from the unit to the outside with the plumber. Note any windows, corners or long runs.
  • 4Condensate and drain. High-efficiency units produce acidic condensate that needs a proper drain or neutralizer.
  • 5Permit and inspection. Ask the Village what your job requires. Do not assume.
  • 6Service plan. Decide who will descale the unit and when. Hard use shortens the interval.

Descaling keeps a tankless unit honest

Mineral scale collects inside the heat exchanger and narrows the passages. The symptoms are slower flow, higher temperatures swinging up and down, error codes and noise. Descaling means flushing the unit with a mild acid solution through isolation valves, which is why installers fit service valves at the start.

Many manufacturers suggest annual or periodic service, with intervals depending on your water. Skipping it voids some warranties and wears the unit prematurely. If your unit has no service valves, a plumber can add them.
