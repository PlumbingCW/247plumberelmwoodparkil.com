Do I Have a Lead Service Line? What Elmwood Park Homeowners Should Know

Your water reaches the house through a pipe most owners have never looked at. Illinois law has made that pipe worth a few minutes of your attention.

By the Elmwood Park 24/7 Plumbing teamPublished October 29, 2026Updated October 29, 2026

The pipe that brings city water from the street into your house is called the service line. It usually runs underground to a point near your water meter, and unless you have a reason to dig, you have probably never seen it. Whether it is copper, plastic, galvanized steel or something older is not something you can tell from the kitchen sink.

For homeowners in Elmwood Park, IL, the question now has a formal answer to go with it. The Village is surveying service line materials under Illinois law. This guide explains how to look at your pipe, what the results can and cannot tell you, and what to do next.

Why a buried pipe became a public project

Illinois passed the Lead Service Line Replacement and Notification Act, which requires water suppliers to inventory their service lines and plan replacement of lead ones. Lead was used for service lines in many American cities in the past because it is soft and easy to bend and join.

Elmwood Park is part of that effort. The Village is gathering information on pipe materials through a Water Service Material Inventory, and it wants input from owners and tenants alike. Your answers help fill gaps, since the stretch of line on your property is often the part officials know least about.

Checking the pipe at your meter, step by step

1

Find where the line enters

Look in the basement or utility area for the meter and the pipe leading to it from the outside wall. The material of the pipe just before the meter is the clue most useful to the inventory.

2

Look at color and shape

Lead tends to be dull gray, soft looking and often thicker at joints where it was wiped with solder. Copper is brownish orange. Galvanized steel is gray and threaded. Plastic is usually black, blue or white.

3

Try the scratch test

Gently scratch a small spot with a coin or key. Lead is soft and shows a shiny silver mark. Do not sand, file or cut, and wash your hands afterward. Scratching lightly keeps dust from being created.

4

Try a magnet

Hold a magnet to the pipe. A magnet sticks to galvanized steel but does not stick to lead or copper. Note what you see and take a photo of the pipe and the meter.

5

Report what you found

Share your observations with the Village through its inventory process. If you cannot tell, say so. A plumber can confirm the material in person.

What your observations may point to

The test is rough. It cannot prove anything, and the pipe may change materials between the street and the house. Use the table to describe what you saw, and let the Village or a plumber confirm.

What Illinois law means if the pipe is lead

State law generally bars partial lead service line replacement, meaning replacing only the owner's side or only the street side. The reasoning is that disturbing a lead line can shake loose particles and leave an uneven joint. In general, a replacement is meant to cover the full line from the water main to the meter.

That is why it matters not to start your own digging. A full replacement is coordinated with the Village, since part of the line lies in the public way. Our water line repair service handles the owner's side of a line and works with the Village where a project involves it.

If your whole house has aging pipe as well, the interior plumbing may need attention at the same time. A repiping plan can be coordinated with a service line project so the walls and the street are not opened twice.

Flushing the tap is not a fix

Running the tap clears stagnant water from your home, but it does not change the pipe. It is not a substitute for finding out what the line is made of. For health questions about your household, ask the Village and your doctor, and do not rely on guesses from a website.

Questions to ask before anyone digs

Ask who is responsible for each section of the line and who coordinates with the Village. Ask whether the work covers the full line from main to meter. Ask for the plan in writing, including how the yard, sidewalk or driveway will be restored.

Digging in Illinois requires a call to 811 at least 3 days and no more than 10 before work starts, and the locate is at no charge. Utilities do not mark private lines, so the plumber has to locate the line on your property. Look up any plumber you hire on the state licence verification page before they begin.

Elmwood Park's service line survey and what it asks of you

Elmwood Park buys Lake Michigan water from the City of Chicago, meters it, stores it in an underground tank and a water tower, and pumps it to homes. It treats and chlorinates the water before delivery and publishes an annual water quality report on its Public Works and Water page. Read it, and test your tap if you want more detail about your own water.

The Village says it has begun steps to remove lead water services in the coming years, and the Village inventory is available on request. We do not know how many lines are affected, and we cannot tell you whether any given house has one. Only the Village inventory and a look at the pipe can answer that.

Home age alone does not settle the question. The median home here was built in 1955, and many are 50 to 90 years old, so older materials are often found, but that is only a hint. Answer the Village survey whether you own or rent, since tenants are asked too.
