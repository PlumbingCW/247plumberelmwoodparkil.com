Every new fixture needs three things behind the wall: water coming in, a drain going out, and a vent that lets the drain breathe. Plumbing installation in Elmwood Park means planning those three lines for the room you want, then connecting the fixture so it works and passes inspection where one is required.

Some installs are a morning job, like a new vanity or a washer hookup. Others are a month of coordination with a general contractor. We handle either, and we explain what we need from you before the first pipe is cut.

Rough-in and finish: the two visits behind most installs

Most remodel plumbing happens in two stages, with other trades working in between.

Rough-in

Before drywall and tile, we run supply, drain and vent lines to the planned fixture locations. This is when walls and floors are open and changes are cheap.

Inspection stage

Where an inspection applies, it usually happens after rough-in and before the walls close. We pressure-test lines so we know they hold before anyone covers them.

Finish

After tile and cabinets, we set the fixtures, connect the shutoff valves, and test every joint with water.

Fixtures that are not new at all: swaps and hookups

A lot of our installation calls are simple. A buyer has a new dishwasher, a landlord is converting a unit, a homeowner finished the basement and now needs the washer connected. We install shutoff valves, supply lines and traps, and replace old S-traps with properly vented P-traps when the layout allows.

If another contractor finished the walls and left plumbing unfinished, call anyway. We connect what is there, test it, and tell you honestly if a pipe someone else installed needs to be corrected before it will pass an inspection.

Bathrooms below grade: why a basement bath is its own project

Many Elmwood Park houses were built decades ago with a basement slab that sits lower than the main drain. A toilet or shower down there may not drain by gravity. Two answers exist: a sewage ejector pit with a pump, or a self-contained upflush toilet system that pumps waste up to the drain line.

Both use a pump, both need a vent plan, and both should be priced against what the Village building department requires. Where basement flooding is a concern, consider the sewer backup information on our sump pump page before you design anything below grade.

Permits and inspections: ask the Village first

Whether a given job needs a permit depends on its scope, so call the Village building department before you commit. The Village follows the Illinois State Plumbing Code, and its plumbing permit application covers flood control items, water heaters and sewer work, among others.

Before a plumber pulls permits, the Village wants a bond, proof of liability insurance and the State licence on file. Fees are paid before work begins, and an inspection takes two working days of notice. Homeowners doing their own work sign a hold harmless form, but that is a lot of paperwork for most people.

Hints that an old house needs more than a hookup

Sometimes the new fixture shows what the old pipes cannot handle.

Galvanized supply that is partly clogged

Low flow at the new faucet may mean the line is narrowed with rust. See repiping.

Drain that sits at the wrong height

A tub or washer standpipe may not reach the trap at a code-friendly height.

Cast iron stack that is rusted through

Cutting into a failing stack changes the scope.

A shutoff valve that will not close

We replace it before we connect anything new.

What affects the price of an install

Price is driven by access, the number of fixtures, pipe material, the distance to the stack, whether walls and floors are open, the fixture you pick, and whether a permit and inspection are part of the job. We explain each factor and state the price before work starts.

If you are not sure what to ask for, describe the room on the phone and we will help you think through it. For emergency fixture trouble, go to toilet repair or faucet repair.
