Cheap checks to run before anyone digs

Many wet basements improve when water is kept away from the house. These checks cost little and rule out the easy causes.

Start outside, then work in

  • 1Downspouts. Make sure each one empties well away from the foundation, not at the wall or into a splash block that slopes back.
  • 2Grading. The soil along the foundation should fall away from the house. A low spot or a settled patch beside the wall funnels rain inward.
  • 3Window wells. Clear leaves and debris, and check for a drain at the bottom. A well with no outlet can fill like a bucket.
  • 4The foil test. Tape a square of foil flat against a damp wall for a day. Moisture on the room side points to humidity. Moisture under the foil points to water coming through.
  • 5The sump pit, if you have one. Pour a bucket of water in and watch the pump start, empty the pit and stop without the water running back.

How drain tile and a sump work, and what they will not do

Interior drain tile is a perforated pipe laid in a trench along the inside edge of the basement floor. Water that reaches the footing runs into the pipe instead of across the floor, travels to a sump pit, and a pump sends it outside. It manages groundwater pressure, which is why it suits seepage at the floor edge.

It will not stop water from entering the wall, it will not cure a cracked wall and it does nothing for sewer backup. It also needs a working pump, so a backup plan matters. Our sump pump page covers pumps, check valves and backup systems in detail.

The work means cutting a channel in the concrete floor, so expect dust, a patched strip along the wall and a few days of disruption depending on the length.

Pipe entries, floor drains and window wells

Water can follow a pipe through the wall. Where a water line, gas line or drain passes through the foundation, the gap around it may have been sealed poorly or have aged. We can reseal those openings with the right material for the pipe and the wall.

A floor drain is the lowest opening in many basements, and in a combined-sewer village it is where sewer water often shows up first. A drain with a backwater valve or a threaded plug helps. A drain that smells or drains slowly may need cleaning, which our drain cleaning team handles.

Elmwood Park facts that affect a wet basement

A few local points shape what makes sense here.

Combined sewers

Storm water and household waste share the Village sewer. Heavy rain can push water back into basements, and a sump pump does not stop that.

Rain barrels and downspouts

The Village promotes the MWRD rain barrel program. MWRD also advises disconnecting downspouts, which reduces what reaches the sewer and the soil beside your wall.

Flood-proofing assistance

The Village has offered cost-sharing for items such as overhead sewers and new drain tile with a sump. Ask the building department whether it is currently open.

Insurance and reporting

Ask your insurer about sewer backup coverage before the next storm, and report flooding to the Village.
