The next ten minutes

  • 1Look at the outlet, not the pump. If the outlet and the floor around it are dry, check whether a GFCI has tripped or a breaker has switched off. If water touches the outlet, leave it and call us.
  • 2Leave the float alone unless it is safe. A float jammed against the pit wall is a common reason a healthy pump stays silent. Unplug the pump before you touch anything near the pit, and never reach into water with the cord live.
  • 3Send less water down the house drains. Hold the laundry, the dishwasher and long showers until the pit calms down. When the sewer is running full, every extra gallon makes the day worse.
  • 4Call the 24/7 line. Tell us the pump's age, whether it hums, runs without emptying the pit, or does nothing, and whether a backup was ever installed. That tells us which pump and parts to bring.

Why pumps quit in the same storms that need them most

Pumps sit idle for weeks, then run hard in the worst weather. Most failures trace to a short list of causes.

  • A float switch that snags on the pit wall or the discharge pipe, so the pump never gets its signal to start.
  • A failed check valve, which lets pumped water fall back into the pit so the motor cycles without making progress.
  • An intake screen or impeller packed with grit and debris.
  • A tripped GFCI or a power outage, which silences a perfectly good pump.
  • A worn-out pump, or one too small for the water the soil now sends.
  • A blocked or frozen discharge line outside, leaving the water nowhere to go.

A pump that runs endlessly without lowering the water points toward the check valve, the discharge or a pump out of its depth. One that never starts points toward power or the switch.

Backup power and backup pumps compared

A pit with one pump and one power source has two single points of failure. Here is how the common backups differ.

Backup options for a sump pit
OptionHow it worksWhere it falls short
Battery backup pumpA second pump with its own battery starts when the main pump fails or the power drops.Runs for a limited time in a long outage and needs testing and eventual battery replacement.
Water-powered backupHousehold water pressure drives a jet that pushes pit water out, no electricity needed.Needs good water pressure, uses a lot of water while running and sends it to a drain, so local rules matter.
GeneratorKeeps the main pump and other circuits alive.Someone must start it, and it must never run indoors or in a garage.

Backwater valves and overhead sewers: flood control for a combined-sewer village

Here, rain and household waste travel together. The Village owns combined sewers, and in a downpour that shared pipe can run full and shove water back toward basements. A sump pump deals with groundwater around the foundation and cannot stop a surcharged sewer, so the two problems call for different tools.

A backwater valve sits on the building drain and shuts when flow reverses. An overhead sewer sends basement waste to a sealed pit and pump that lifts it above the floor, so the sewer must rise much higher before it reaches a fixture. A damaged building drain is another matter, covered under sewer line repair.

The Village building department has offered a cost-sharing program for flood-proofing work: overhead sewers, backflow valves paired with a bypass pump, lift stations, and new drain tile leading to a sump. Its plumbing permit form lists similar items. Whether the program is open right now is a question for that office, which can also say if an inspector will look over your setup. Permit rules vary by job.

The record explains the interest. Flooding disasters were declared for the area in 2007, 2008 and 2011, and in July 2011 rain of 2 to 4 inches an hour left several feet of water on Oak Leaf Avenue. A project to reduce sewer backups finished in 2015. We never promise that a valve or pump prevents flooding. They lower the risk when someone tests them.

Sump pit or sewage ejector pit?

A sump pit collects groundwater from drain tile around the foundation. A sewage ejector pit, found where a basement bathroom sits below the sewer line, collects waste and has a sealed, vented lid. They look alike, but the pump, lid and safety rules differ, so tell us which you have.
